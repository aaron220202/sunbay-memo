A. Top Recommendation

Passon the round as proposed

Today Sunbay sells automated payment reminders. The company worth backing would be an AI that actually collects the money, and that has not been proven yet.

Sunbay solves a real problem for buyers with budget: chasing late payments costs European businesses an estimated €275B a year, and its revenue is growing about 15% a month (my estimate) across 20+ customers. But what it sells today, automated email and text reminders, is a feature that Polish invoicing apps and banks already offer, and Poland's new mandatory e-invoicing system (KSeF) gives them the same invoice data. The part that could make Sunbay a large company, an AI voice agent that negotiates payment, is mentioned in its funding announcement but not on its product pages or in its customer stories, and it is the uncertainty that matters most for the return. $6M is a fair market price but too high for us: I estimate the expected return at 2.2x against the 4.4x our fund needs, a level this deal reaches only at about a $3M valuation (section 6). I would revisit at the next round if, within 18 months, three things are true:

  1. The voice agent works: across at least 10 paying customers, those using it get paid at least 10 days sooner.
  2. Prices rise: average revenue per customer above €700 a month (about €8,400 a year).
  3. Customers expand: existing customers spend at least 10% more over 12 months (net revenue retention above 110%).
Where I could be wrong
  • Arguments about how big a company can get are often wrong at this stage. My Pass rests on Sunbay's ceiling, and good teams often find a bigger market than their first deck describes. Chaser is one company, in one country, built before AI; it is evidence, not proof.
  • AI could change what small-firm software can charge. If the voice agent does even half of a collections clerk's job, it is worth well over €700 a month to a Polish company (my estimate), and my 30% chance of higher pricing is too low. This is the belief I am least sure of.
  • The two mistakes don't cost the same. Investing wrongly loses $800K, about 1% of the fund. Passing wrongly could miss the one company that returns it. That is why I propose a small first investment with pro-rata rights rather than walking away: it keeps us in the deal for a quarter of the money.

B. Key Arguments & Risks

Why we could invest

  1. Customers get clear value. One customer says Sunbay saves it 40+ hours a week. At €290 a month, Sunbay charges roughly a tenth of the staff time it saves (my estimate, at €15 an hour).
  2. It is growing fast. Revenue grew about 7x in 14 months (my estimate), roughly 15% a month: the low end of what investors look for at this stage (section 5).
  3. Central and Eastern Europe is under-served. Upflow, Kolleno and Tesorio are priced for larger companies, and the cheap UK tools connect to Xero rather than Polish accounting software.
  4. Poland's new e-invoicing system makes setup easier. Since 2026, Polish business invoices are recorded digitally in KSeF (large firms from February, most others from April, the smallest from January 2027), so Sunbay could connect to a customer's invoices in one step.
  5. The team learns fast. It narrowed a vague "automation platform" down to invoice collection and brought in Kogito, s20 and Kahoot's CTO as investors.
Upside case 17–26x
Sunbay becomes the AI collections assistant for mid-sized European companies: it calls customers who owe money in their own language, agrees payment plans and charges a share of the cash it recovers. Selling through accounting firms, as Visma's SaldeoSMART reached 55,000 companies through 1,500 of them, it reaches €30–50M of annual revenue and sells for $200–300M.

Key risks

  1. Today's product is a feature others already offer. Fakturownia already sends automatic text reminders for overdue invoices; wFirma and PKO BP offer similar tools.build what they won't: AI calls that negotiate, payment plans and dispute handling.
  2. The e-invoicing system helps competitors too. Every Polish accounting app now sees the same invoice data.win on results and on collecting from customers abroad, not on data access.
  3. Collections software for small firms rarely grows large. Chaser, a leading UK tool, has about $3M of revenue after 12 years.sell to firms with 50–250 staff and charge a share of cash recovered.
  4. The most important feature is unproven. The June 2026 announcement says Sunbay makes AI voice calls in several languages, but its product pages and customer stories don't show them.make proven voice results a condition of any investment.
  5. Small customers are expensive to win. Sunbay has added about one customer a month since April 2025 (my estimate, from 5–6 customers to 20+). At €3,500–5,000 a year per customer, it cannot afford a salesperson to win each one, yet the realistic case in Exhibit 1 needs ~28 new customers a month.sell through accounting firms and banks, which already reach thousands of small companies.
Downside case 0–1x
Sunbay stays a useful tool for Polish small businesses with €1–3M of annual revenue, loses ground to free reminders, and sells for $5–20M. Later investors get paid first, so we get our money back at best.

C. Section Analyses

1. Team

  • The founders know the problem first-hand. The CEO has 9 years in operations and automation, including at YC-backed Plane; the COO 5 years in finance and operations, including at Droppe; the CTO 5 years in software engineering and finance, including at mBank. The deck describes the pain like people who have lived it. Their experience is in running finance and operations inside companies, though, not in selling software to them.
  • Ownership is clean, but they committed late. The Polish company register shows Sunbay sp. z o.o. was set up in Warsaw on 19 March 2025, with the three founders as its only directors and shareholders. But two founders went full-time only in April–May 2025, and the deck listed the CTO's start date as "[SOON]". Before investing I would confirm vesting, the equity split and when the CTO started.
  • No one is responsible for sales, and it shows. Selling to small businesses depends on finding customers cheaply and repeatedly. Founder-led selling has added about one customer a month (section 5); the realistic case needs ~28. A sales or partnerships lead is the first hire I would push for.
  • Their "generalist" approach cuts both ways. It helped them refocus on collections quickly, but it also led to plans for side products before the core product was working.
  • My overall view: founders worth backing, not yet a proven team. They have passed the hard first test, finding a problem companies pay to solve. They have not yet passed the second, selling it repeatedly. That gap is normal at pre-seed, but at a $6M price I would expect it to be closing already.

2. Product

  • What works today is easy to copy. Automated email and text reminders, late-payment interest notes, invoice scanning and bulk invoicing are useful but standard. Customers likely choose Sunbay today for easy setup and the founders' hands-on help, not for software others can't build.
  • The AI voice agent is what would make Sunbay valuable, and it is unproven. It would turn a reminder tool into something that does a collections clerk's job. The funding announcement says it makes AI calls, but the product pages describe calls as tasks for staff. The voice technology itself is cheap (about $0.08–0.10 a minute from ElevenLabs), so Sunbay's advantage would have to come from how well its calls negotiate and the payment data it builds up.
  • Calling a business is harder than calling a consumer. When one company owes another, the person who answers is usually in accounts payable, and the invoice is often late because of a missing order number, a disputed line or an approval queue, not an unwilling payer. An AI that finds the right person and clears the dispute is valuable; one that repeats the reminder by phone is not.
  • Customers will worry about the AI upsetting their best clients. The customers who pay late are often a firm's biggest. Before letting an AI phone them, buyers will want control over who gets called and how. That will make adoption slower than the deck implies, and controls of this kind may matter as much as the voice itself.
  • What I would tell the founders: focus and charge more. Getting paid 10 days sooner frees about €270K of cash for a company with €10M of revenue, worth ~€22K a year at an 8% borrowing cost (my estimate). That supports €700+ a month, or a share of the cash recovered, instead of a flat €290. They should drop the planned side products (document requests, contract renewals) and sell through accounting firms.

3. Market

  • Late payment is a lasting problem. About 11% of European revenue is paid late, and the EU's proposed 30-day limit on payment terms has stalled, and the European Commission is reported to be withdrawing it.
  • The money is in medium-sized firms. In the EU, firms with 50–249 staff are 14% of the targets in Exhibit 1 but 31% of the market's value; in Poland, almost half. They have more overdue invoices worth chasing and more reason to pay for results. Small firms are easier to sign but pay less and tend to leave sooner, so Sunbay should aim higher than it does today.
  • Poland alone is worth about €180M a year. That is too small for a very large outcome, so Sunbay must win in other countries early. Its customers already chase payments in 30 countries, which helps.
  • E-invoicing laws are spreading, which helps Sunbay expand and helps local rivals. KSeF became mandatory for most Polish firms in 2026 (the smallest follow in January 2027); France requires it from September 2026 (for large and mid-sized firms; small firms from 2027) and Germany from 2027–28. If Sunbay builds its product around KSeF, each new law is a chance to repeat the approach in another country. But each country also has its own invoicing apps with the same data. The deck mentions none of this.
  • The EU is worth about €4.3B a year, but a realistic five-year result is about €8M of annual revenue. That supports a good sale to a larger company, not a fund-sized return. The calculation is set out step by step in Exhibit 1.

4. Competitive Landscape & Differentiation

  • The deck compares Sunbay with the wrong companies. It names Tesorio, Collbox, Zapier and Make. In Poland, the real competition is the invoicing app or bank the customer already uses: Fakturownia already sends automatic text reminders for overdue invoices.
  • The likeliest buyers are also the likeliest competitors. Three kinds of company could buy Sunbay. Accounting software groups: Visma has bought Poland's SaldeoSMART, invested in the Polish invoicing app inFakt, and bought a Norwegian debt collector, Creno. Banks: PKO BP already runs its own collections tool for small firms. And debt-collection agencies, which could want software that catches invoices before they turn into bad debt (my view). The first two are also best placed to build AI collection themselves. Whether they build or buy decides between a near-zero and a $40–80M outcome, so the competition question and the exit question are the same question.
  • AI collection companies could move in. Murphy ($15M raised, backed by ElevenLabs) and JustSolve (€3.7M raised) use AI voice agents to collect debts today. Moving to business invoices would be a small step for them.
  • Sunbay's chance to stand out: AI collection built for Poland and its neighbours, though its data advantage would come late. No one clearly offers it yet. The usual claim is that payment data becomes a lasting advantage, but early on it is weak: in a fragmented market few of Sunbay's customers chase the same debtors, so the data only becomes valuable across thousands of customers. It is a reward for winning, not a way to win, which brings the question back to selling.
  • My prediction: that chance closes by the end of 2027, despite a reason for incumbents to hold back. By then I expect a Polish invoicing app or bank to launch AI collection built on the KSeF data. The best argument against this: an invoicing app often serves both the firm sending an invoice and the firm that owes it, so an AI that chases debtors hard risks upsetting its own users, and every collection call carries reputational risk for a bank. That may slow them, but it will not stop them, because they can offer gentle AI reminders without phoning anyone. I still put the chance they move first at 65%, so Sunbay has to prove the voice agent with this round's money, not the next one's.

5. Traction & Benchmarking

  • Growth is strong, from a very small base. Annual recurring revenue grew from ~$16K in April 2025 to ~$110K by June 2026 (my estimate, from 20+ customers paying €300–500 a month). That is about 15% a month, the low end of the 15–20% that investors look for before the next major round (Series A). It is still about a tenth of the ~$1M a year at which Series A conversations usually start, and in April 2025 consulting brought in twice as much as the software.
  • Most growth came from new customers, and they arrive slowly. Revenue grew ~7x: about 3.5x more customers, each paying about twice as much. Higher prices are encouraging, because they suggest room to charge more. But new customers arrive at about one a month, against the ~28 a month the realistic case needs.
  • The deck measures activity, not money, so I estimated the unit economics. Its main metric is the number of automated messages sent. Running the voice agent is cheap: about €35–40 a month in voice charges for a mid-sized customer (150 three-minute calls at ElevenLabs' rates, my estimate), around 5% of an €800 price. AI-model and phone-line costs come on top, but even doubling the total leaves healthy margins. The constraint is the cost of winning each customer. Software for small businesses should earn that cost back within 12 months; at €290 a month and ~80% margins, Sunbay can spend at most ~€2,800 per small customer: too little for sales visits, workable through accounting firms. At €800 a month the limit rises to ~€7,700, which is another reason to aim at medium firms. Investors will also want net revenue retention (Bessemer's benchmarks: 100% good, 110% better, 120%+ best) and cash recovered per customer.
  • Growth slows as companies get bigger. If monthly growth slows by 30% each year (my assumption; even the fastest software companies slow as they grow), Sunbay reaches $1M of annual revenue in early 2028 and about €10M in early 2034. That is a company sold to a larger buyer, not one that returns the fund.
  • The customer references are friendly startups. Tequipy, the main customer story, is itself a VC-backed startup. A win with a traditional logistics or energy company would be stronger evidence. With about 20 customers, losing one costs ~5% of revenue.

6. The Round & Returns

  • The terms: $1.2M at a $6M post-money valuation. The deck itself names no terms; I treat this as the round on offer today, coming after the €550K Sunbay raised in June 2026 (see 6.2). That values the company at $4.8M (about €4.4M) before the money comes in, and new investors get 20%. That is below the ~€6M median pre-money valuation for European pre-seed and seed rounds, so the price is fair for the market. But it is about 55 times today's revenue, so we would be paying for the voice agent working, not for today's business. At my estimates the deal only reaches the return our fund needs at about a $3M valuation, half the price asked.
  • Stake we would target: $800K for 13.3%, with $800K held back. The second $800K is kept to invest in the next round. As we usually do, we would commit early rather than lead, help Sunbay find a lead investor, and ask for pro-rata rights (the right to invest in later rounds to protect our stake). Taking the full $1.2M (20%) would put too much of the fund into a deal I rate below our return target. One thing to check: Sunbay raised €550K in June 2026, but the company register still lists only the founders as shareholders, which suggests it was a convertible investment that will reduce our stake later.
  • Potential returns: returning the whole fund needs a ~$1B sale. Three more funding rounds of ~18% each, plus 5% set aside for employee shares, would reduce our 13.3% to ~7%. At 5 times annual revenue, a $1B sale means ~$200M of revenue, or roughly 42,000 customers at an average of €4,350 a year (Exhibit 1). Private software companies sold for 3.1–4.0 times revenue in 2026. The one large sale in this area, Billtrust for $1.7B, was a broad billing and payments platform, not a reminder tool.
  • What would need to hold: six things, all at once. A voice agent that measurably speeds up payments, customers paying €8–10K a year, sales through accounting firms, existing customers spending 10%+ more each year, real revenue outside Poland by year three, and a buyer willing to pay 5+ times revenue. Each is a high bar on its own: since 2021, fewer than 30% of companies raising a seed round have reached a Series A within two years, and Sunbay is a stage earlier. Without these, the most likely good outcome is a sale to Visma or a Polish bank within 3–4 years for $40–80M, roughly 3.5–7 times our money.
  • What I would offer instead: $200K now, more once the conditions are met. We would invest $200K (3.3%) through a SAFE, a simple agreement that converts into shares later, capped at a $6M valuation, with pro-rata rights and our commitment to help line up a lead investor once the three conditions in section A are met. This is consistent with my view that $6M is too high: a small investment is not a bet on today's business but the price of the right to invest more once the voice agent is proven. If Sunbay fails, we lose $200K, about 0.3% of the fund. Sequoia used a similar step-by-step approach with YouTube: $1M first, then $4M once targets were hit.

How the model works, and the deal assumptions

If the voice agent doesn't work, Sunbay stays a reminder tool: 75% chance it shuts down, 25% it is sold for a small amount. If it works but competitors launch AI collection first, Sunbay is squeezed: 40% shuts down, 35% small sale, 25% sale to a larger company. If it works and Sunbay stays ahead, pricing and international growth decide the size: with both, it becomes a European leader (65%) or returns the fund (35%); with one, it is mostly sold to a larger company; with neither, a small or mid-sized sale. Sale values: small $15M, to a larger company $40–80M, European leader $300M, fund-returning $1B. The 4.4x target comes from standard fund terms: 2% yearly fees for 10 years and 20% of profits to the fund managers mean the fund must make 4.4 times the money it invests to give its investors 3 times their money.
